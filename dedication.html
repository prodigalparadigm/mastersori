Behind the name

Named November 17, 2026.
By Kathleen Bartin.

Two imperfect women built durable methods out of institutions that underestimated them. I chose the name for both of them. I am interested in the conditions they made possible, not in polishing either life into a parable.

Meaning before machinery

Margaret Masterman

1910-1986 · LANGUAGE & COMPUTATION

A philosopher and pioneer in computational linguistics, Masterman founded the Cambridge Language Research Unit, which worked outside Cambridge’s formal university structures with limited computing resources.

Her work emphasized semantics and the meaning of language rather than treating syntactic analysis as the whole problem; the unit explored thesauri as tools for translation and other language-processing tasks. Her papers in the ACL Anthology.

She also wrote “The Nature of a Paradigm,” the essay that counted at least twenty-one senses in which Thomas Kuhn used the word.

She was one of the founders of Lucy Cavendish College, a Cambridge college founded for mature women, and its first Vice-President.

I named the method in part for a woman who asked what a paradigm was good for. Here is a fair question for mine: what can someone else do with it that I did not teach them to do?

The part I carry forward: ordinary language is not the consolation prize for people who cannot code.

In Mastersori, the learner writes the project as a story: beginning, middle, end, with enough detail for another reader to follow the same path. That is my application of the idea, not a claim that Masterman designed today’s prompting methods.

The conditions for independence

Maria Montessori

1870-1952 · MEDICINE & EDUCATION

Montessori was an Italian physician and educator whose method grew through observation of children acting freely within an environment prepared for their needs. Her classrooms gave learners accessible materials, freedom of movement, and room to choose and repeat purposeful work.

The educator’s role was to remove obstacles and support development, with independence as an aim rather than constant direction as the default.

The part I carry forward: prepare the environment so the learner can act.

That means approved tools that work, permissions already resolved, a useful first task, and space to try again. It does not mean treating adults as children. It means respecting the adult learner enough to make independence the point.

Without the mythology.

Montessori’s son Mario spent his early years in the care of others, apart from his mother; they were reunited when he was a teenager, and he later became a close collaborator. The pressures on an unmarried professional woman belong in that account, but so does the separation itself.

She cooperated with Mussolini’s government, which promoted Montessori education, before the relationship broke down; she resigned from the Opera Montessori in 1933 and left Italy in 1934. The earlier patronage is not erased by the later break.

A dedication becomes less trustworthy when it edits out the parts that resist admiration. I can learn from the work without making a claim that either woman was beyond criticism.

The connection is the method.

Masterman gives the name its attention to meaning. Montessori gives it the prepared environment. I bring the work of organizational AI adoption: the governance conversation, the frightened department head, the first broken connector, and the project someone finally believes they can build.

Prepare the conditions.
Make the meaning clear.
Help people do it themselves.
